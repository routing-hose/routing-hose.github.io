Abstract

Routing hoses and tubing requires predicting dynamics for hollow deformable linear objects (HDLOs) that are difficult to capture reliably with any single simulator. We present R-HDLO, a framework for goal-conditioned hose routing that learns predictive dynamics across heterogeneous physics formulations and adapts them to previously unseen hoses through interaction. R-HDLO trains an object-centric graph dynamics model using data from AVBD, XPBD, articulated capsule-chain, and FEM simulators, then infers and refines a latent mechanics representation from observed hose interactions. Under a matched total training-data budget, multi-physics HDLO training reduces node RMSE across held-out simulation backends by 9.6% relative to the best single-source model and by up to 16.4% across single-source models. We integrate the adapted dynamics model with sampling-based model predictive control for closed-loop routing. In simulation, R-HDLO achieves 81.3% routing success across multiple hose mechanics and routing goals, compared with 25.0% for the best single-source learned model and 20.8% for the fixed-parameter direct-physics reference. On the physical system, R-HDLO achieves 62.5% average success across two routing goals while enabling replanning in approximately 1.07 s per step, over 280× faster than direct simulation.

R-HDLO learns predictive hose dynamics across heterogeneous physics simulators, adapts a latent mechanics representation from robot interactions, and uses the adapted model for goal-conditioned receding-horizon planning on the physical system.

Routing does not require recovering the true simulator-specific parameters of a hose. The robot needs a predictive representation of mechanics, inferred from how the hose responds to interaction, that predicts the consequences of candidate actions. R-HDLO exports every simulator's states to the same hose centerline graph and learns one forward model from all of them, conditioned on a latent code z that is initialised from probing interactions and refined online from observed transitions. Task objectives are defined separately as costs over predicted hose configurations, so the same learned predictor serves different boards and goals.

Multi-Physics Hose Simulation

The same kind of bimanual action, two kinematic grasps moved through three Cartesian waypoints, applied to hoses in four backends that represent a hose in very different ways. Each clip replays one recorded transition from the simulator's own state at its native resolution, with two YAM arms posed by inverse kinematics.

One representation, four physics models

Each backend keeps its native discretization, deformation and contact model; every settled state is exported as an ordered set of material stations along the centerline, so a single graph model can learn from all of them. Every world samples hose length, radius, density, bending and stretch stiffness, damping and friction, the initial shape (straight, curved, periodic or random), the boundary condition and the peg layout.

Learned Hose Dynamics

The model predicts the next settled centerline as an analytic kinematic base plus a graph-network residual, conditioned on nearby peg surface points and a 16-D mechanics latent z. An interaction encoder initialises z from observed transitions; at deployment the network weights are frozen and only z is refined.

Adapting to the Hose at Hand

Before routing, the robot performs six probing interactions at two fixed interior grasp locations, alternating the moving arm. The encoder turns them into an initial code z0; z is then refined by Adam through the frozen dynamics model, regularised toward z0, and refit after every usable routing action from at most the 24 most recent interactions. No simulator parameters are estimated.

Planning with MPPI

At every step MPPI samples 1,024 two-phase bimanual action sequences, rolls them out through R-HDLO with z fixed, scores the predicted hose against the goal's centerline and peg contract, and refines the sampling distribution over three iterations (3,072 sequences, 6,144 predicted transitions). A final check compares the weighted-mean plan, the best sample and not moving; the first phase of the chosen plan is executed and the robot replans from the observed hose. A step takes about 1.07 s, against 302.6 s for direct AVBD rollouts.

Notes on the Videos and Results

  • Tables come from the paper. Prediction metrics are means ± population SD of world-level scores for one fixed trained model per row (not across training seeds). Routing entries are success rate / mean final normalized routing cost.
  • Real-world videos. The Blender half of every real-world video is rebuilt from that run's log: the hose is the tracker's centerline and the arms are posed by the rig's own inverse kinematics from the recorded commands. The Goal 1 video shows one attempt that the operator stopped after five moves without routing every peg; it illustrates the setup and the planner, not a successful trial. The three-peg route video shows a later run on a rearranged board that routed all three pegs; its arm timing is rebuilt from the session log, which records every move and gravity-sag trim but not how long the arms took to settle, so that one number is fitted per move to the overhead film.
  • Arms in simulation are for visualisation. Simulated grasps are kinematic; the YAM arms are posed by inverse kinematics on the recorded gripper motion. An arm that cannot reach a waypoint, or would intersect the other arm, is drawn faded.
  • Simulated hose motion. Settled states are recorded by the executing simulator. During moves the hose is re-simulated where the replay matches the record within 2 mm and linearly interpolated otherwise (always for FEM).
  • Clips and interactive viewers come from earlier recorded runs and checkpoints and are chosen to show the method clearly; the tables report the paper's evaluation.
  • Limitations. Manipulation is quasi-static (actions begin and end at settled configurations), the hose state comes from an RGB-D centerline estimate rather than contact sensing, adaptation needs six probing interactions before routing, and the physical routing experiments cover two hoses and two goals.
